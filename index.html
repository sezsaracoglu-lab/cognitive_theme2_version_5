🧵 Synthesis

One thesis for the whole theme: short-term memory is small, fragile and active. Part A shows its limits: a few chunks (7 ± 2, or about 4), lost within seconds without rehearsal, shaped by position (primacy and recency) and by interference from similar meaning. Part B replaces the single short-term store with a working-memory system: a phonological loop, a visuo-spatial sketchpad (visual cache and inner scribe), an episodic buffer for binding, all steered by a capacity-limited central executive with separable functions. Dual-task interference, double dissociations and patients reveal the parts. Part C shows why it matters in a classroom: all components relate to children’s calculation, but visuospatial short-term memory adds unique predictive power, which calls for assessment and explicit connections between visual and symbolic math.

How the pieces connect: chunking (A) = binding in the episodic buffer (B) · rehearsal (A) = the articulatory loop (B) · interference (A) = dual-task interference (B) · capacity limits (A, B) = individual differences that predict math (C).

⏱️ Short-term memory: a fragile, limited store

Why the new colleague’s name disappears

  • Some memories are so fragile they ‘evaporate before you even begin to use them’, even after a delay of less than a minute (Paas & Kester, 2006).
  • Short-term memory: holds a small amount of information recently taken in from the environment; limited in duration and in amount.
  • Long-term memory: large capacity; experiences and information accumulated over a lifetime.
  • Attention is limited too (Chapter 3): attention limits how much passes on to memory, and STM limits how much reaches further processing.
  • These limits were first framed as limits of short-term memory; working memory, an updated view, comes in Part B.

🧩 Miller’s magical number seven and chunking

How much can short-term memory hold?

  • Miller (1956): we can hold about seven items, give or take two (5–9) in short-term memory.
  • The unit is the chunk: a memory unit of several components strongly associated with each other (Schwartz, 2011); STM holds about seven chunks.
  • Chunking raises capacity: phone number 617-346-3421 becomes six chunks (617 + 346 + 4 digits) if the area code and college prefix are familiar.
  • Unusual for its time: in the era of behaviourism, Miller proposed internal mental processes that recode stimuli.
  • Cowan (2005): pure capacity, without chunking, is closer to four; Miller inspired classic STM research.
🪝 Memory hook: 7 ± 2 boxes 📦, but you choose how big each box is.
★ KEY INSIGHT · STM capacity is limited in chunks, not raw items, so chunking with prior knowledge expands what we can hold.
Tap to open ▸
Your short-term memory has room for about seven ‘things’. The trick is that a thing can be a single digit or a familiar group like ‘617’, so grouping lets you fit far more.

🔢 The Brown/Peterson and Peterson technique

Half of three letters gone in five seconds

  • Two methods dominated STM research in the 1950s–70s: the Brown/Peterson and Peterson technique and the serial-position effect.
  • Brown (1958) and Peterson & Peterson (1959) independently showed that material held for less than a minute is often forgotten.
  • Procedure: study three unrelated letters (CHJ), then count backward by threes from a three-digit number (prevents rehearsal), then recall.
  • First trials: most letters recalled. After several trials, earlier letters interfered; after a mere 5 s, about half were forgotten (Figure 4.1).
  • Showed STM is fragile; inspired hundreds of studies and boosted the cognitive approach (Bower, 2000).
🪝 Memory hook: No rehearsal + old lists = gone in 5 seconds 💨.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Without rehearsal and with interference from earlier trials, short-term memory loses information within seconds.

📉 The serial-position effect

Primacy, recency and a U-shaped curve

  • Serial-position effect: a U-shaped relationship between an item’s position in a list and its probability of recall (Rundus, 1971; still found today).
  • Recency effect: better recall of the last items, thought to be still in short-term memory at recall and not moved to permanent memory.
  • So STM size can be estimated by counting the recalled end items: about three to seven items (Davelaar et al., 2005, 2006).
  • Primacy effect: better recall of the first items because (1) they don’t compete with earlier items and (2) people rehearse them more.
  • Middle items are recalled least accurately.
🪝 Memory hook: First 🥇 and last 🏁 are remembered; the middle gets lost in the crowd.
★ KEY INSIGHT · The serial-position curve reveals two influences: rehearsal and lack of competition (primacy) and items still in STM (recency).

🍎 Meaning matters: release from proactive interference

Wickens et al. (1976)

  • Proactive interference (PI): previously learned material interferes with new learning (XCJ, IBR, TSV make KRN hard).
  • Release from PI: switching category (letters → shapes or numbers) brings recall back almost to the first-trial level.
  • Wickens et al. (1976): three Brown/Peterson trials with words from one category (occupations, meats, flowers, vegetables or fruits), 18 s counting; trial 4: three fruits for everyone.
  • Predictions by semantic similarity to fruit, all confirmed: fruits worst, vegetables poor (edible, from plants), meats and flowers better (one shared attribute), occupations best.
  • So semantic similarity determines the amount of interference; STM capacity depends on chunking and word meaning.
🪝 Memory hook: Same drawer, crowded 🗄️; new drawer, fresh start 🆕.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Interference in STM depends on meaning: the more similar new items are to old ones, the more they suffer.

🖥️ The Atkinson–Shiffrin model

An information-processing view of memory

  • Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968) proposed a classic information-processing model: mental processes resemble a computer’s operations, and information passes through stages, one step at a time.
  • Memory = a sequence of separate steps; at each step information is transferred from one storage area to another (Figure 4.4).
  • External stimuli first enter sensory memory, which records information from each sense with reasonable accuracy.
  • Attended information enters short-term memory (limited); rehearsal keeps it there and transfers it to long-term memory.
  • This multi-store model gives a classic explanation of the serial-position curve (next lecture) and was later challenged by working memory.
🪝 Memory hook: A conveyor belt 🏭: senses → STM → LTM, pushed along by rehearsal.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Atkinson and Shiffrin describe memory as stores in sequence, with rehearsal moving information from STM to LTM.

🧭 Explaining the serial-position curve

Stores and interference

  • Multi-store explanation (Atkinson & Shiffrin): primacy = early items rehearsed most and transferred to LTM; recency = last items still in STM at recall.
  • Matlin: early items are easy because they don’t compete with earlier items and get more rehearsal; recency items were ‘still in short-term memory’.
  • Interference explanation: proactive interference (earlier → later) and retroactive interference (later → earlier).
  • First items suffer no PI, last items no RI, middle items suffer both → the U-shape.
  • background a filled delay before recall removes recency but not primacy (Glanzer & Cunitz, 1966): evidence for separate stores.
🪝 Memory hook: Middle items are sandwiched 🥪 between interference from both sides.

🚀 How to increase what we can remember

Chunking, rehearsal and meaning

  • Chunking (Miller, 1956): recode several items into one meaningful unit using long-term knowledge (617-346-3421 = six chunks).
  • Rehearsal: silently repeating items keeps them available; preventing it (Brown/Peterson) causes rapid loss.
  • Reduce interference: varying the category or meaning of what you learn releases proactive interference (Wickens et al., 1976).
  • Exploit position: important items at the start or end of a list benefit from primacy and recency.
  • Preview of Part B: short and phonologically distinct words are easier (word-length and phonological similarity effects).

🔦 Unitary-store vs multi-store models

Is STM simply activated LTM?

  • Unitary-store models: short-term memory is the currently activated part of long-term memory; the hippocampus and medial temporal lobes might serve both.
  • Race et al. (2013): face recognition after 8-s or 15-s delays: damage limited to the hippocampus → intact; more extensive medial temporal lobe damage → impaired.
  • Evaluation: activated LTM plays an important role in STM, and the MTL may contribute.
  • Limitations: STM is more than activation, since we manipulate information (backward digit recall, new images; Logie & van der Meulen, 2009).
  • Amnesic patients show much greater hippocampal involvement in LTM than STM, and imaging finds little hippocampal activity when STM and LTM are separated (Bergmann et al., 2012) → multi-store view.

🧠 From short-term memory to working memory

Baddeley & Hitch’s multicomponent model

  • Phone numbers are now stored in phones; Baddeley & Hitch (1974) argued STM is useful for complex tasks that need processing and brief storage (mental arithmetic).
  • They replaced the short-term store with working memory; the latest version has four components (Baddeley, 2012).
  • Central executive: modality-free, limited capacity, ‘an attentional system’; the most important component; handles any demanding task.
  • Phonological loop (speech-based, preserves order) and visuo-spatial sketchpad (visual and spatial) are slave systems.
  • Episodic buffer: temporary storage of integrated information from the other components (added 25 years later; Baddeley, 2000).

♟️ Dual-task studies: Robbins et al. (1996)

Which components does a task use?

  • Two assumptions: (1) two tasks using the same component can’t be done well together; (2) tasks using different components can be done as well together as separately.
  • Robbins et al. (1996): weaker and stronger chess players chose continuation moves while doing a secondary task.
  • Secondary tasks: repetitive tapping (control), random number generation (central executive), pressing keys clockwise (sketchpad), saying ‘see-saw’ = articulatory suppression (phonological loop).
  • Move quality fell with visuo-spatial and central-executive tasks, not with articulatory suppression → chess uses the CE and sketchpad, not the loop.
  • Effects were similar for stronger and weaker players → both groups use working memory the same way.
🪝 Memory hook: If two jobs need the same worker 👷, both slow down.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Selective dual-task interference reveals which components a task uses and that the components are separable.
Tap to open ▸
To find out which parts of working memory chess uses, players had to say ‘see-saw’, tap keys in a pattern, or call out random numbers while thinking. Only the spatial and the random-number tasks hurt their chess.

🗣️ The phonological loop

Store, rehearsal and the phonological similarity effect

  • Two parts (Figure 6.5): a passive phonological store linked to speech perception, and an articulatory control process linked to speech production (rehearsal).
  • Spoken words enter the store directly; visually presented words need articulation to get in.
  • Phonological similarity effect: immediate serial recall is worse for similar-sounding words (FEE, HE, KNEE…) than dissimilar ones (BAY, HOE, IT…): 25% worse (Larsen et al., 2000).
  • More complex than assumed: semantic processes contribute (Acheson et al., 2010); the model is underspecified (Schweppe et al., 2011).
  • Schweppe et al.: the effect depends more on acoustic than articulatory similarity; articulatory similarity mattered for spoken but not written recall.

📏 The word-length effect

Evidence for rehearsal, and a confound

  • Word-length effect: word span (words recalled immediately in order) is greater for short than long words (Baddeley, 2012).

  • Jalbert et al. (2011): confound with orthographic neighbourhood (words differing by one letter); short words have more neighbours.
  • Equating neighbourhood size made the effect disappear → some doubt about the word-length effect itself.

🧷 The episodic buffer

Where information gets bound together

  • The episodic buffer holds integrated information (chunks) in a multi-dimensional code combining visual, auditory and other sources; a buffer between WM components and perception and LTM. Capacity ≈ four chunks.
  • Why added? The original components were too separate; it stores verbal and visual information together; and immediate recall is ~5 unrelated words but up to 16 in sentences (Baddeley et al., 1987).
  • Baddeley & Wilson (2002): immediate prose recall needs buffer capacity + an efficient CE; amnesics with little CE deficit recalled prose much better than those with severe executive deficits.
  • But Baddeley et al. (2009): a CE task hurt sentence and word recall equally; Allen et al. (2012): counting backwards hurt single features and feature combinations equally → binding is automatic.
  • Current view (Baddeley, 2012): an essentially passive structure where bindings achieved elsewhere can be displayed; unclear how verbal and visual inputs combine; smell/taste untested.

📖 Working memory capacity

Reading span, operation span and intelligence

  • Working memory capacity: how much information an individual can process and store at the same time; higher capacity → higher intelligence and attentional control.

🧵 Synthesis

One thesis for the whole theme: short-term memory is small, fragile and active. Part A shows its limits: a few chunks (7 ± 2, or about 4), lost within seconds without rehearsal, shaped by position (primacy and recency) and by interference from similar meaning. Part B replaces the single short-term store with a working-memory system: a phonological loop, a visuo-spatial sketchpad (visual cache and inner scribe), an episodic buffer for binding, all steered by a capacity-limited central executive with separable functions. Dual-task interference, double dissociations and patients reveal the parts. Part C shows why it matters in a classroom: all components relate to children’s calculation, but visuospatial short-term memory adds unique predictive power, which calls for assessment and explicit connections between visual and symbolic math.

The working-memory demands of mathematics probably change because the content and teaching of math change and because skills become automatised. C4

Symptom‘Why caution, and what instead?’: an applied recommendation.
DiagnosisCorrelation ≠ causation; transfer; explicit connections; assessment-based intervention.
TreatmentGive the reasons for caution → then concrete recommendations backed by the paper.

  • Exposure ≠ transfer: children with weak visuospatial skills may not connect manipulatives to paper-and-pencil calculation.
  • WM training alone doesn’t improve math (Cunningham & Sood, 2018).

🪝 Memory hook: Blocks 🧱 + words 🗣️ + symbols ✍️, connected on purpose.
★ KEY INSIGHT · Giving children blocks isn’t enough. Some can’t link the blocks to the sum on paper. A teacher should show the connection step by step and check which skills each child needs help with.
Tap to open ▸

Teachers should be cautious for several reasons. First, the study is correlational and cross-sectional, so it cannot show that the sketchpad causes math skill or that visuospatial activities improve it. Second, simple exposure to visuospatial materials (diagrams, number lines, base-ten blocks) does not guarantee transfer: children with relatively weak visuospatial abilities, who are exactly the ones struggling, may be unable to connect these activities to other math tasks such as paper-and-pencil calculation. 🧩B.5

The Episodic Buffer, the Model’s Verdict, and Working Memory Capacity

❓ This lecture answers: Why add a buffer, how good is the model overall, and how do people differ in capacity?
B.5.1
Why add an episodic buffer?

🧩 Integrated chunks in a multi-dimensional code, about 4; links WM to perception and LTM (Baddeley, 2012). Added because the parts were too separate, and sentences beat word lists (5 vs 16).

B.5.2
Does the buffer need the executive?

🔗 Baddeley & Wilson (2002): yes, for prose. But an executive task hurt sentences and lists equally (Baddeley et al., 2009); feature binding is automatic (Allen et al., 2012). Now: a passive display.

B.5.3
The working memory model overall

✅ Beats Atkinson-Shiffrin: processing + storage, explains partial patient deficits, rehearsal optional, durable. ❌ Oversimplified (Postle, 2006), executive unclear, interactions unknown.

B.5.4
Working memory capacity

📐 Process + store at once: reading span (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980), operation span. Lower when anxious (Eysenck et al., 2007), higher after relaxation (Kargar et al., 2013); r ≈ +0.6 with intelligence, mainly fluid (Unsworth, 2010).

Math difficulties, and the words used for them

C.1.2
Three suspects: attention, phonological processing, working memory

🔎 ADHD often co-occurs (11–33%); MD + RD struggle with word problems; working memory often named the core deficit. Model as three parts; attention is built into working memory (Baddeley, 2002; Cowan, 2008).

C.1.3

🧪 Three meta-analyses all implicate the sketchpad (David, 2012: stronger in younger children). AWMA (Alloway et al., 2004): 12 subtests. Alloway & Passolunghi (2011): loop at 7, sketchpad at 8.

🏫C.3

What It Means, and What It Does Not

❓ This lecture answers: Why only the sketchpad, what about attention and phonology, and what should teachers do?
C.3.1
Why only the sketchpad?

🤔 (1) Meta-analysis categories lacked visuospatial STM (Swanson & Jerman, 2006); (2) visuospatial parts can’t be separated (Miyake et al., 2001); (3) age: loop → sketchpad → executive (David, 2012; Alloway & Passolunghi, 2011).

C.3.2
Attention and phonological processing

🎯 Attention dropped out: parent report, and in Cowan’s (2008) model STM is the focus of attention. Phonology stayed: maths is taught with language; reading link (Cirino et al., 2018; Slot et al., 2016).

C.3.3
Verbal and visual together

🔢 Dehaene’s triple code (verbal, visual, written); Geary’s three subtypes (facts, visuospatial, procedures). Infants grasp small quantities (Wynn, 1992); number sense predicts maths (Huttenlocher et al., 1994).

C.3.4
Teaching, assessment and limitations

🏫 Not longitudinal → no cause. Not just manipulatives; WM training alone fails (Cunningham & Sood, 2018); combined helps (Fuchs et al., 2022). No non-verbal control; only calculation measured.

📄 Theme 2 on one page

7️⃣A1

Limits of STM

About 7 ± 2 chunks (Miller), ≈ 4 without chunking (Cowan). Group items into familiar units: knowledge expands memory.
⏱️A2

Measuring it

Block rehearsal and half of three letters is gone in 5 s. Lists give a U-curve: primacy (rehearsed) and recency (still in STM).
🍎A3

Meaning & stores

Old items interfere with new ones; a new category releases it, more so the less similar (Wickens). Atkinson-Shiffrin: stores in sequence.
♟️B1

Working memory

Memory for doing complex tasks: executive, loop, sketchpad, buffer. Dual tasks show which part a task uses (chess: executive + sketchpad).
🔁B2

Phonological loop

Sound store + inner voice. Similar sounds and long words hurt; silencing the voice removes the word-length effect. Needed for new vocabulary.
🗺️B3

Sketchpad

What (visual cache) and where (inner scribe). Specific interference with easy tasks, general with hard ones.
🎛️B4

Central executive

Attentional, stores nothing. Inhibition, shifting, updating share a goal-keeping core. Damage hits task setting, monitoring, energisation, metacognition separately.
🧩B5

Buffer & capacity

Binds ≈ 4 chunks (16 words in a sentence vs 5 alone). Capacity (reading/operation span) predicts fluid intelligence (r ≈ .6).
🧒C1

Maths: the puzzle

3–7% of children struggle; attention, phonology and WM are suspects. The AWMA tests all four WM parts.
📊C2

The finding

94 children: all four parts correlate with calculation, only the sketchpad adds unique variance (10.2%).
🏫C3

What it means

Maybe age (loop → sketchpad → executive). Not causal: link visuals explicitly to written maths, don’t just add manipulatives.
